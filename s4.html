Quadratic models describe situations where the rate of change is itself changing — a ball thrown upward, a company's profit as a function of price, or the area of a rectangle with a fixed perimeter. To look inside these models, we must learn to solve their equations.

Factorisation is the process of rewriting an expression as a product of simpler factors. It is the reverse of expanding, and it serves as our first and most elegant tool for solving quadratic equations. When an expression factors cleanly, this method allows us to find solutions efficiently and mentally — far faster than using heavy algebraic formulas.

1. Common Factoring & Notable Identities

The absolute first step when factorising any mathematical expression is always to inspect the terms for a common factor — a single variable or numeric term that divides cleanly into every component of the expression. This technique utilizes the standard distributive property of multiplication in reverse:
$$ab + ac = a(b + c)$$
Once any global common factors are extracted, we look for structural short-cuts. Certain algebraic expansions appear so frequently across mathematics that they are highly worth recognizing on sight. These predictable structures are called notable identities:
  • Square of a sum: $a^2 + 2ab + b^2 = (a + b)^2$
  • Square of a difference: $a^2 - 2ab + b^2 = (a - b)^2$
  • Difference of squares: $a^2 - b^2 = (a - b)(a + b)$
Worked Example 1: Factorise $4x^4 + 2x^3 - 5x^2$.
Each term contains $x^2$ as a common factor. Factoring this out gives:
$$4x^4 + 2x^3 - 5x^2 = x^2(4x^2 + 2x - 5)$$
The remaining factor does not simplify further.
Worked Example 2: Factorise $16x^2 - 49$.
We recognise the difference of squares pattern with $a = 4x$ and $b = 7$:
$$16x^2 - 49 = (4x)^2 - 7^2 = (4x - 7)(4x + 7)$$

2. The Third Identity

When a quadratic expression is monic — meaning the coefficient of $x^2$ is exactly $1$ — we can bypass longer algebraic algorithms entirely. Instead, we can factorise the expression rapidly through inspection by looking for a specific arithmetic pattern.

To factorise a quadratic expression of the form $x^2 + bx + c$, we look for two numbers, $a$ and $b$, whose product equals the constant term $c$ and whose sum equals the middle coefficient $b$:
$$x^2 + (a + b)x + ab = (x + a)(x + b)$$
Worked Example 3: Factorise $x^2 - 6x + 5$.
We need two numbers $a$ and $b$ such that $a + b = -6$ and $ab = 5$. Testing: $a = -1$ and $b = -5$ gives $(-1) + (-5) = -6$ ✓ \& $(-1)(-5) = 5$ ✓.
Therefore:
$$x^2 - 6x + 5 = (x - 1)(x - 5)$$
When the coefficient of $x^2$ is not $1$ and cannot be factored out cleanly, we use a method called factoring by grouping. Instead of guessing factors for the entire expression at once, we look for a way to split the middle term into two parts, allowing us to factor the polynomial in pairs.

To factorise a non-monic quadratic $ax^2 + bx + c$, find two numbers whose product equals $ac$ (the leading coefficient multiplied by the constant) and whose sum equals $b$:
$$\text{Product} = a \cdot c \qquad \text{and} \qquad \text{Sum} = b$$
Once found, rewrite the middle term $bx$ using these two numbers and factorise the expression by grouping terms term by term.
Worked Example 4: Factorise $9x^2 + 24x + 7$.
Since the leading coefficient is not $1$, we use the grouping method. We begin by looking for two numbers whose product equals $a \cdot c$ ($9 \cdot 7 = 63$) and whose sum equals $b$ ($24$). Testing factors of $63$, we find that $3$ and $21$ work perfectly because $3 \cdot 21 = 63$ and $3 + 21 = 24$. We use these two numbers to split the middle term, $24x$, into two parts:
$$9x^2 + 3x + 21x + 7$$
Next, we group the terms into pairs and factorise the greatest common factor out of each pair:
$$= 3x(3x + 1) + 7(3x + 1)$$
Now, notice that both terms share a common binomial factor of $(3x + 1)$. Factoring this out gives our final simplified answer:
$$= (3x + 1)(3x + 7)$$
