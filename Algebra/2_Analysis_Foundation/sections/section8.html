8. The Chain Rule

Architectural Overview

When computing rates of change for nested systems, you cannot find the overall derivative simply by differentiating the parts independently. Imagine a domino ripple effect: changing the deepest variable shifts the inner layer, which then forces the outer wrapper to change.

💡 THE OUTSIDE-IN MENTAL MODEL:

Think of a composite function like a wrapped gift. To find how fast the total system changes, you use a two-step process:
Step 1: Differentiate the Outside Wrapper (leaving the contents untouched).
Step 2: Multiply by the rate of change of the Inside Contents.

🧮 LEIBNIZ MATRIX DERIVATION

Let y = f(u) and u = g(x). A minor fractional shift Δx forces an immediate adjustment in the inner layer Δu, which ripples into the final output value Δy:

ΔyΔx = ΔyΔu · ΔuΔx

Taking the limit as Δx → 0 transforms these delta relationships into exact differential calculus equations:

dydx = dydu · dudx
🚨 THE COMPOSITE TRAP — OVERLOOKING INNER CHAINS:

The derivative of a nested function is never equal to the derivative of the outside layer on its own: [f(g(x))]' ≠ f'(g(x)). Forgetting to multiply by the inner loop derivative multiplier g'(x) completely breaks your calculation velocity when finding physics optimizations or geometric tangent lines down the line.

🌍 THE UNIVERSAL NESTING PROTOCOL:

While the two profiles below are the most common algebraic variants you will face, the Chain Rule works exactly the same for any nested function. Whether you have an algebraic expression trapped inside a trigonometric shell sin(g(x)), an exponential layer eg(x), or a logarithm ln(g(x)), you always differentiate from the outside-in: derive the outer shell first, then multiply by the inner derivative g'(x).

📦 PROFILE A: POWERS OF BRACKETS

When a base polynomial expression is trapped inside an integer power exponent:

[(g(x))n]' = n · (g(x))n-1 · g'(x)
  1. Bring the master power exponent n to the very front.
  2. Decrease the bracket's power layer by exactly 1.
  3. Multiply everything by the internal derivative g'(x).
🧪 PROFILE B: RADICAL RADIX CORES

When a variable expression is nested securely underneath a classic square root sign:

√g(x)' = g'(x) 2√g(x)
  1. Compute the clean internal derivative g'(x).
  2. Drop that derivative directly into the numerator position.
  3. Set the denominator to exactly two times the original parent root.

⚠️ Note: This structural proof is completely optional for core exam completion, but highly recommended for system architects wanting to observe Leibniz conversion thresholds.

Let independent shift parameters approach zero bounds. We evaluate the dynamic conversion fractions step-by-step by multiplying the standard expansion array with a matching middle-variable fraction:

ΔyΔx = ΔyΔu · ΔuΔx

Because the inner function layer g(x) is perfectly continuous across its structural limits, forcing the baseline change to zero constraints (Δx → 0) simultaneously locks the middle change array into immediate zero boundaries (Δu → 0):

limΔx→0 ΔyΔx = (limΔu→0 ΔyΔu) · (limΔx→0 ΔuΔx)

These distinct analytical limits convert smoothly into canonical differential equations, verifying why the external wrapper scale matches internal multiplier factors flawlessly: dy/dx = (dy/du) · (du/dx).

Example 9.2: Quotient Protocol with Power-Bracket Chain Rule Layers

Differentiate the rational fraction featuring a composite exponent denominator base:

h(x) = x(3x - 2)2
Solution Steps:
  1. Identify Numerator and Denominator: Let u(x) = x → u'(x) = 1.
    Let the denominator factor be v(x) = (3x - 2)2.
  2. Evaluate the Denominator Chain Rule: Differentiate v(x) by drawing the power forward and multiplying by the inner core derivative (3): v'(x) = 2(3x - 2)1 · (3) = 6(3x - 2).
  3. Assemble the Quotient Formula Layer: Substitute into u'v - uv'v2:
    h'(x) = (1) · (3x - 2)2 - x · [6(3x - 2)][ (3x - 2)2 ]2

    h'(x) = (3x - 2) · [ (3x - 2) - 6x ](3x - 2)4 = (3x - 2) - 6x(3x - 2)3

    h'(x) = -3x - 2(3x - 2)3
