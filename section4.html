Section 4 — Factorisation

Quadratic Models and Factorisation

Quadratic models describe situations where the rate of change is itself changing — a ball thrown upward, a company's profit as a function of price, or the area of a rectangle with a fixed perimeter. To look inside these models, we must learn to solve their equations.

Factorisation is the process of rewriting an expression as a product of simpler factors. It is the reverse of expanding, and it serves as our first and most elegant tool for solving quadratic equations. When an expression factors cleanly, this method allows us to find solutions efficiently and mentally — far faster than using heavy algebraic formulas.

Common Factoring & Notable Identities

The absolute first step when factorising any mathematical expression is always to inspect the terms for a common factor — a single variable or numeric term that divides cleanly into every component of the expression. This technique utilizes the standard distributive property of multiplication in reverse:

ab+ac=a(b+c)

Once any global common factors are extracted, we look for structural short-cuts. Certain algebraic expansions appear so frequently across mathematics that they are highly worth recognizing on sight. These predictable structures are called notable identities:

  • Square of a sum: a2+2ab+b2=(a+b)2
  • Square of a difference: a2−2ab+b2=(a−b)2
  • Difference of squares: a2−b2=(a−b)(a+b)
Worked Example 1: Factorise 4x4+2x3−5x2.
Solution: Each term contains x2 as a common factor. Factoring this out gives:
4x4+2x3−5x2=x2(4x2+2x−5)
The remaining factor does not simplify further.
Worked Example 2: Factorise 16x2−49.
Solution: We recognise the difference of squares pattern with a=4x and b=7:
16x2−49=(4x)2−72=(4x−7)(4x+7)

The Third Identity

When a quadratic expression is monic — meaning the coefficient of x2 is exactly 1 — we can bypass longer algebraic algorithms entirely. Instead, we can factorise the expression rapidly through inspection by looking for a specific arithmetic pattern.

To factorise a quadratic expression of the form x2+bx+c, we look for two numbers, a and b, whose product equals the constant term c and whose sum equals the middle coefficient b:

x2+(a+b)x+ab=(x+a)(x+b)

Specifically, find two numbers a and b such that:

a+b=coefficient of x and ab=constant term

Remark: This identity connects naturally to the earlier ones: if b=0 it reduces to common factoring; if a=−b it reduces to the difference of squares since a+b=0.

Worked Example 3: Factorise x2−6x+5.
Solution: We need two numbers a and b such that a+b=−6 and ab=5.

Testing: a=−1 and b=−5 gives (−1)+(−5)=−6 ✓ and (−1)(−5)=5 ✓.

Therefore: x2−6x+5=(x−1)(x−5)

When the coefficient of x2 is not 1 and cannot be factored out cleanly, we use a method called factoring by grouping. Instead of guessing factors for the entire expression at once, we look for a way to split the middle term into two parts, allowing us to factor the polynomial in pairs.

To factorise a non-monic quadratic ax2+bx+c, find two numbers whose product equals a⋅c (the leading coefficient multiplied by the constant) and whose sum equals b:

Product=a⋅candSum=b

Once found, rewrite the middle term bx using these two numbers and factorise the expression by grouping terms term by term.

Worked Example 4: Factorise 9x2+24x+7.
Solution: Since the leading coefficient is not 1, we use the grouping method. We begin by looking for two numbers whose product equals a⋅c (9⋅7=63) and whose sum equals b (24).

Testing factors of 63, we find that 3 and 21 work perfectly because 3⋅21=63 and 3+21=24. We use these two numbers to split the middle term, 24x, into two parts:
9x2+3x+21x+7 Next, we group the terms into pairs and factorise the greatest common factor out of each pair:
=3x(3x+1)+7(3x+1)
Now, notice that both terms share a common binomial factor of (3x+1). Factoring this out gives our final simplified answer:
=(3x+1)(<3x+7
